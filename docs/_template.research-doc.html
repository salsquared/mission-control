Findings at a glance
Research-doc convention (delete before publishing). Six sections are required: Findings at a glance (first) → Motivation & scope → Prior art → Open questions → Confidence ledger & gaps → Sources (last), plus a Glossary immediately before Sources whenever the doc carries field-specific vocabulary (§11). The tail order is fixed. The four middle sections are optional — include them when they apply. Three rules distinguish this archetype from a design doc: (1) every OQ carries a Settled by: line naming the concrete evidence that would resolve it — this is the research analogue of a task list, and it is what stops open questions becoming permanent debate; (2) the Confidence ledger separates cited findings from the author's own inference, so a later reader never inherits a guess as established fact; (3) the Glossary defines every esoteric or field-specific term from the literature, with the source named per entry — a survey imports a vocabulary along with its findings, and an undefined term is an unverified one. There is no Tasks section and no Rollback section — nothing is being sequenced and nothing is deployed. If you find yourself wanting either, the doc has graduated and wants the design-doc template.

Summary

Five to eight bullets, each a finding rather than a topic — state the conclusion, not the subject area. Research docs get skimmed; anything a reader must know before forming an opinion belongs here, not on page four. Cross-reference the section that argues each one.

  • <The most surprising or most load-bearing finding>. <One or two sentences of substance — the claim and why it matters> (§n).
  • <A finding that inverts an intuitive framing>. <What people assume, and what is actually true> (§n).
  • <A hard limit the reader must internalize>. <What it forecloses> (§n).
Epistemic status. State plainly how well-grounded the doc is: roughly how many sources back it, which sections are survey vs. original reasoning, and a pointer to §10.1 for the claims that are inference rather than published finding. A reader who stops after §1 should still know how much to trust it.

Motivation & scope

The question, and why it's live

What gap prompted this — grounded in the current system or the current state of the art, not vibes. What does not working on it cost? A research doc earns its keep by making a fuzzy question precise, so spend the words here getting the question right.

Scope boundaries

Unlike a design doc's goals/non-goals, this is not a delivery commitment — it is a map legend. Say what the doc covers, what it deliberately excludes, and — critically — whether it is one track of a larger program. If so, banner it:

Every other option in §n is unevaluated.

What remains unsolved by anyone

Keep this subsection. Distinct from §9, which lists your decisions — these are open problems in the field. It is the highest-value part of a survey: it tells the reader which of their questions have no answer to find, and it is where a genuine contribution would sit. Flag any requirement of yours that sits outside the literature entirely — that is a research gap, not a lookup you haven't done yet.

Integration surface (optional — when it feeds an existing system)

Where this could attach

Exploratory, ordered by risk to the existing system — not a staging commitment (that is the design doc's job). A table of options with a risk column, and any structural constraint that applies to all of them.

Adversarial surface (optional — when trust or incentives matter)

Attack surface

What a motivated adversary does if this ships, and what counters it. Table: attack · mechanism · countermeasure.

Non-adversarial failure

Keep these separate from attacks. Benign causes — measurement error, legitimate change, drift, misconfiguration — are usually more likely than adversaries and are systematically under-weighted when mixed into the same table.

Open questions & what would settle them

Decision forks

Same OQ discipline as the design doc — one OQ = one decision, lettered mutually-exclusive options, each self-contained (name the approach, the concrete change, the effect, then Pros: / Cons:), and a trailing Lean: naming the pick by letter first with at least one sentence weighing the trade-offs. Split bundled sub-decisions into separate OQs. Deciding an OQ appends, never replaces: keep the options list, swap Lean: for ✓ Decided., flip the banner to callout tip.

The research-doc addition: every OQ ends with Settled by: — the concrete evidence, measurement or lookup that would resolve it, stated specifically enough to act on. "Benchmark it" is not a Settled by; "reconstruct one capture twice with different seeds and compare hash stability of the derived artifact" is. This replaces the design doc's Tasks section, and it is the single highest-value habit in the archetype: it converts arguments into experiments, and it surfaces which questions are cheap lookups masquerading as decisions — those should always be resolved first.

Glossary (required whenever the doc uses field-specific terms)
The rule: if a term is esoteric, or reads like an ordinary English word but is not, it gets an entry — and the entry names its source. A survey imports a vocabulary along with its findings, and that vocabulary is where borrowed error hides: an undefined term is an unverified one, and a term used loosely for three sections becomes a term the next reader redefines by guessing. Two triggers, either sufficient:
  • Field-specific jargon — an acronym or coinage that means nothing outside the literature: ERGAS, Fmask, Bulyan, MNF, FWHM.
  • Vague literal meaning — the trap case, and the one most often missed. An everyday word carrying a narrow technical sense that a reader will otherwise supply from general English: registration is alignment, not enrolment; harmonisation is a fitted per-sensor transform, not agreement; radiometry is the values rather than the positions; a medoid is not a median. These read as understood and are therefore never looked up.

Entry rules — the same markup as the glossary archetype (_template.glossary.html), with one addition:

  • Every entry names its source, inline, in the closing <span class="see">: Source: + a link to the primary paper, standards document or reference implementation the definition was taken from. Prefer the originating work (Kruse for SAM, Zhu & Woodcock for Fmask) or the agency specification (ESA, USGS, NASA) over a secondary explainer. This is what separates a glossary from a set of remembered paraphrases — and it lets a later reader check a definition without re-running the search.
  • Definitions come from the literature, not from recall. If no source can be found for a term, that is itself a finding — say so in the entry rather than inventing a definition.
  • Where the doc's own argument extends, qualifies or leans on a definition, say so and cross-reference the section (“…which is the mechanism behind OQ4”). The glossary is the doc's vocabulary, not a general dictionary.
  • Sources cited only for a definition live here rather than being repeated in Sources; anything load-bearing for a finding belongs in both.
  • Group along whatever spine the doc uses (the processing chain, the approach families of Prior art), alphabetical within a group. One <h3> per group, each followed by its own <dl class="glossary">.
  • Anchor every <dt id="g-slug"> so entries deep-link, and cross-link siblings inline with <a href="#g-slug">.
  • If the project keeps a canonical glossary.html, that one wins on shared vocabulary — define here only what is specific to this survey, and point at the project glossary for the rest.
